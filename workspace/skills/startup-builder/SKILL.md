# Startup Builder

Use this skill for startup strategy, customer discovery, positioning, MVP design, pricing, launch planning, sales operations, and cold outreach.

The objective is not to produce a long strategy document. Help the founder make the next high-leverage decision with explicit evidence, a small experiment, an owner, and a deadline.

## Decision standard

- Separate **facts**, **customer quotes**, **assumptions**, and **recommendations**. Do not upgrade an assumption into evidence.
- Name uncertainty and quantify it where useful. If current evidence is weak, recommend discovery before building.
- Use the narrowest useful customer segment. "Small businesses" is not an ICP; describe role, company type, trigger, current workflow, buying ability, and reachability.
- Optimise for learning velocity before scale. A manual concierge workflow is often a better first test than software.
- Never invent market size, customer proof, revenue, conversion, competitors, contacts, or legal/compliance conclusions.
- End a decision with a clear `continue`, `change`, or `stop` threshold.

## Operating method

1. Frame the opportunity: customer, painful job, trigger, current workaround, and specific segment. Do not start with features.
2. Build an assumption map: desirability, viability, feasibility, distribution, and trust/risk. Identify the riskiest assumption first.
3. Choose the smallest test that can disprove it: five interviews, landing-page intent test, paid design-partner offer, concierge pilot, or prototype usability test.
4. Convert results into a decision. Do not call a test successful merely because people say they like the idea; look for behavior, urgency, access, and willingness to commit.
5. Define an MVP as the shortest path to one valuable user outcome. Explicitly list what is out of scope, manual, or deferred.
6. Give founder-ready outputs: ICP, problem statement, value proposition, proof, experiment, owner, deadline, success threshold, and next decision.

## Founder brief template

Use this compact structure whenever enough information is available:

```text
Customer / trigger:
Pain and current workaround:
Evidence we have:
Riskiest assumption:
Positioning: For [ICP] who [job], [product] helps [outcome] unlike [alternative] because [credible reason].
Next experiment:
Success threshold:
Owner / deadline:
Decision if threshold is missed:
```

## Customer discovery

- Seek recent, concrete behavior: "Tell me about the last time..." beats "Would you use...".
- Ask about trigger, workflow, time/cost of the problem, alternatives, buyer/approval process, priority, and what has already been tried.
- Do not pitch during the first part of an interview. Summarize the problem back and ask for correction.
- Record exact quotes, context, and confidence level. Protect personal data and do not retain unnecessary sensitive information.
- After 5–10 comparable conversations, summarize patterns and contradictions. Update the ICP rather than averaging incompatible segments.

## MVP and product delivery

- Define one primary user, one trigger, one promised outcome, and one activation event.
- Build the "must be true" path first: acquisition → first value → repeat value → payment or committed next step.
- Prefer instrumented slices over feature lists. Every MVP should specify the event that proves activation and the interval that proves retained use.
- Mark every item as `must have`, `manual workaround`, `later`, or `not doing`. Avoid roadmap inflation.
- Before declaring delivery complete, test the actual happy path and one likely failure path with a representative user or realistic data.

## Pricing and go-to-market

- Anchor pricing in economic value, urgency, alternatives, and buying friction—not build cost alone.
- Test willingness to pay with a real ask: paid pilot, letter of intent, deposit, budget-owner introduction, or price-specific proposal.
- Choose one primary acquisition motion for the next cycle: founder-led outbound, warm introductions, partnerships, community, content, product-led, or paid demand capture.
- Define the funnel in observable stages: targeted accounts → contacted → replies → qualified conversations → proposals → paid pilots → retained customers.
- Report counts and rates separately. Do not combine positive replies, meetings, and revenue into one "conversion" metric.

## Cold outreach

## Cold outreach

- Research the recipient and personalize with a real, relevant observation. Never invent a connection, result, customer, or testimonial.
- Write concise, respectful messages with a clear reason for contacting them, a relevant problem hypothesis, and one low-friction call to action.
- Ask permission before contacting people, sending messages, or uploading lead data. Respect opt-outs and applicable anti-spam/privacy rules.
- Do not mass-message, scrape private data, evade platform limits, or misrepresent identity. Offer an opt-out in email campaigns.
- Start with a small, approved batch. Iterate on segment and positioning before increasing volume.
- Keep a suppression list and stop immediately on an opt-out, complaint, or request not to be contacted.
- Report delivery, reply, positive-reply, qualified-meeting, proposal, and customer rates separately.

### Outreach message structure

```text
Subject: [specific, non-clickbait reason]

Hi [name] — I noticed [truthful observation].
Teams like yours often run into [narrow problem] when [trigger].
We are testing whether [specific outcome] would be useful; [one credible, truthful proof point if available].
Would a 15-minute conversation next week be useful, or should I send a short summary instead?

[name / company / simple opt-out where appropriate]
```

Do not add a claimed result unless it is verifiable. Do not send the message without explicit user authorization and recipient review where practical.

## Metrics and review cadence

For each weekly business review, report:

- Evidence collected: interviews, experiments, customer quotes, and commitments.
- Funnel counts and rates, with the denominator visible.
- Product: activation, time-to-value, repeat use, failures, and support burden.
- Economics: price tested, gross margin assumptions, acquisition effort, and sales-cycle observations.
- Top three risks, what changed this week, and the one decision due next.

Use a metric only if it can change a decision. Vanity metrics such as impressions or sign-ups are supporting signals, not proof of product-market fit.

## MVP review checklist

- Who has the problem, how frequently, and what event makes it urgent?
- What observable outcome proves value?
- What manual or existing workaround is being replaced?
- What is the smallest build that tests the core promise?
- What must be true for a user to pay, return, or refer?
- What decision will the next experiment enable?
- What would make us stop, narrow the segment, or change the offer?

When information is missing, ask only the question that most changes the next decision.
